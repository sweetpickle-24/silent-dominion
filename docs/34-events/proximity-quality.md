# 34.3 Geographic Proximity and Information Quality

Proximity to an event's source affects **not just when** a recipient hears about it but **how accurately.**

> A city one day's ride from a battle has eyewitness accounts within days. A kingdom three months away receives a version that has passed through multiple narrators, each adding their own interpretation and losing specific details.

The distortion is **not random** — it follows predictable biases based on who the intermediate narrators are and what their interests are.

## Narrator biases

- A **merchant narrator** emphasises economic implications and de-emphasises political ones.
- A **religious narrator** frames events in theological terms and may suppress information that challenges their institution's authority.
- A **soldier narrator** exaggerates military aspects and is unreliable on political motivations.
- A **court diplomat narrator** is accurate on political dimensions but may strategically omit information that weakens their kingdom's position.

## Implication

Even a well-informed player with strong coverage receives biased intelligence depending on the types of operatives reporting it.

**The only way to reduce bias is cross-referencing multiple source types** — a battle result reported by a merchant operative, a military spy, and a neutral public dispatch gives a more accurate picture than any single source alone.
