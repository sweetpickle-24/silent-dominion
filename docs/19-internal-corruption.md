# 19. Internal Corruption — When the Machine Betrays You

Every person in the player's organisation has their own interests. Most of the time those interests align with the player's. Sometimes they do not.

- A coordinator who handles large financial flows and has high greed and low loyalty is a corruption risk.
- A lieutenant under sudden personal financial pressure is a corruption risk.
- An operative who has been in place too long without oversight is a corruption risk.

**The organisation that the player builds to protect them is also the organisation that can quietly bleed them dry.**

Corruption is not a random tax on resources. It is a simulation of how organisations actually fail — driven by the same trait model that governs everything else in the game, producing specific events with specific causes that the player can detect, investigate, and respond to.

## 19.1 Corruption profiles and risk factors

Every member of the organisation has a **background corruption risk** derived from their trait scores and circumstances. This risk is not static — it changes as their personal situation changes.

A trustworthy coordinator who accumulates personal debt, receives a blackmail threat, or sees a more lucrative opportunity becomes a risk they were not before. The system tracks personal circumstances of key organisational members and surfaces risk flags before they become events, if the player is paying attention.

### Risk factors

| **Risk factor** | **What drives it** | **Corruption type most likely** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **High greed + financial access** | Trait score + position handling money or bribes | Financial skimming — siphoning a percentage of funds before they reach their destination |
| **Personal financial crisis** | Sudden debt, medical crisis, blackmail from outside | Opportunistic theft — a one-time or short-term diversion of funds during a crisis |
| **Low loyalty + rival contact** | Loyalty trait degraded + rival society approach detected | Intelligence leak — selling operational information rather than stealing money |
| **Ambition + perceived ceiling** | High ambition trait + blocked advancement within the organisation | Operational sabotage — deliberately underperforming to create leverage or a vacancy above them |
| **Long tenure without oversight** | Years in post without audit or cross-check | Gradual drift — small liberties compound over time into significant diversion |
| **Ideological shift** | Regional weight shifts have changed the person's beliefs since recruitment | Value misalignment — working toward different ends without necessarily stealing |

## 19.2 How corruption manifests — silent then visible

Corruption begins **silently.** The skimming coordinator does not announce themselves.

> The financial flows arrive at their destination but at reduced value. The bribe reaches the target at 70% of the intended amount — which means the target is less convinced than their profile suggested. The player reads this as the target being more resistant than expected and adjusts their approach based on wrong data. The corruption is invisible. Only its downstream effects are visible, and those effects look like normal operational variance.

Over time the effects compound and the signals become harder to explain away. Eventually the contradiction is undeniable — but by then the corruption may have run for years.

### The detection chain

1. **Downstream misfire** — an operation produces a result inconsistent with its inputs. Could be normal variance. Flag for monitoring.
2. **Financial discrepancy** — a cross-reference between what the coordinator reported spending and what the financial network recorded being transferred shows a gap.
3. **Pattern** — multiple misfires in the same coordinator's area of responsibility over time. Variance becomes improbable.
4. **Source audit** — the coordinator themselves is investigated. Their personal financial situation, contacts, and lifestyle are examined against their known income.
5. **Confirmation** — direct evidence of diversion, contact with rivals, or deliberate underperformance is established.

## 19.3 Structural corruption prevention

The player can build oversight into the organisation's architecture rather than relying entirely on detection after the fact. Structural prevention costs bandwidth but creates resilience.

- **Cross-checking coordinator** — a second coordinator in the same region whose role includes verifying financial records against operational outcomes. Creates friction and cost but catches discrepancies early.
- **Audit cycles** — periodic reviews of financial flows and operational results in each region, run by a superior or a dedicated auditor operative. Deters opportunistic corruption through visibility.
- **Rotation policy** — moving operatives and coordinators between posts before they accumulate enough tenure to drift. Prevents long-term relationship formation with local actors that enables corruption.
- **Compartmentalised budgets** — limiting how much any single coordinator handles at once. Reduces the scale of any individual corruption event even if it cannot prevent them entirely.

## 19.4 Response options

| **Response** | **What it involves** | **Risk and consequence** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Quiet reassignment** | Move the person to a lower-risk role with less financial access | Safest. They may understand why and become resentful, but they have no dramatic leverage. |
| **Supervised continuation** | Keep them in post under increased oversight while investigation completes | Moderate risk. Allows further evidence gathering. Risky if they detect the surveillance. |
| **Confront through superior** | Their lieutenant addresses the discrepancy directly | Useful if the corruption is opportunistic and the person is fundamentally loyal. Dangerous if they are deeply compromised — a cornered person may defect or expose what they know. |
| **Financial leverage** | The player knows about the theft and uses that knowledge to extract compliance | Creates a dependent under duress. Useful short-term. A person being blackmailed within your own organisation is a permanent instability. |
| **Clean retirement** | Gradually reduce their role, making the ending feel natural rather than punitive | Slowest but leaves the least residue. Only viable if the corruption has not yet reached a level where they feel threatened. |
| **Removal** | The person is neutralised | Highest exposure. Justified only if they are actively dangerous and alternatives are exhausted. A removed coordinator always leaves questions. |

## 19.5 Corruption by rival societies

**Rival societies do not only corrupt the player's organisation from outside — they can actively induce corruption from within.**

Identifying a coordinator with a high-greed trait and approaching them with a better offer is a standard rival operation. The coordinator does not need to defect entirely — they only need to skim in a specific direction, delay a specific operation, or provide a single piece of intelligence at a critical moment.

- A rival-induced corrupt coordinator is **more dangerous than an opportunistic one** because they have direction — their corruption serves specific enemy objectives.
- The **detection signature is different**: rival-induced corruption tends to produce targeted misfires in specific operations rather than broad financial leakage.
- Identifying that a coordinator is rival-induced rather than opportunistically corrupt opens the **double-agent option** — the same mechanics as [intelligence rechecks](./18-intelligence-rechecks.md).
- The player can run the same operation in reverse: identifying a rival society's coordinator with corruption risk and approaching them.
