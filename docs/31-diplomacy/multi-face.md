# 31.4 The Multi-Face Mechanic — Competing Institutions Under Unified Control

The most powerful diplomatic tool in the game is the **hidden common ownership of apparently competing institutions.**

> A king who distrusts Bank A and switches to Bank B, **not knowing both answer to the same immortal**, has made himself more dependent on the player than he was before. He thinks he made a smart independent decision. He has walked deeper into a structure he cannot see.

## The two-bank example

| **Step** | **What happens** |
| --- | --- |
| **1** | The player controls Bank A (established, trusted, holds significant royal debt) and Bank B (smaller, newer, positioned as a trustworthy alternative). |
| **2** | The player engineers a trust collapse in Bank A — a scandal, a missed payment, a rumour of insolvency seeded through merchant networks. |
| **3** | The king, under financial pressure, switches his crown debt to Bank B. Bank B offers better terms to make the switch attractive. |
| **4** | The king now owes Bank B, which the player controls more directly and more discreetly than the now-tainted Bank A. |
| **5** | Bank A is quietly rehabilitated or wound down. Its historical trace — years of royal service — remains a cover for the player's operations during that period. |
| **Result** | The king has never been more dependent on the player, and believes he made the decision himself. |

## Scaling the pattern

This pattern — **multiple faces serving one agenda** — scales across every institutional type:

- Two churches in schism, both secretly guided by the same ideological lieutenant.
- Two scholarly academies in apparent rivalry, both seeding the same idea toward the same cultural destination.
- Two merchant guilds competing visibly while their financial flows both route through the same banking house.

**The player is not one actor in the world. They are many actors playing against each other, all answering to the same immortal.**
